Horror and ThrillerThe Last RainApril 21, 2023Read ↗

It was raining heavily. The curtains swayed in every direction with the wind. The rain had taken away the silence of the evening, making its gentle presence felt all around. Every now and then, the strong wind carried a few drops of rain onto the man sitting quietly on the veranda. He looked lost in thought. He was as lonely as the old house he had inherited from his ancestors, with its walls slowly losing their plaster. His eyes looked tired, and the illnesses of old age had begun to take over his body, one after another.

Suddenly, his wife appeared at the doorway.

"Why are you sitting here getting wet? Come inside."

"I like getting wet in the rain, Sulekha. Would you like to join me? Shall we go up to the roof?"

"Have you lost your mind at this age?"

The man didn't reply. He got up from the veranda and walked toward the front door. From there, he made his way to the stairs, with Sulekha beside him. Holding each other's hands, they slowly climbed to the rooftop. But strangely enough, the rain had stopped by the time they reached the top.

"Are you feeling sad?" Sulekha asked.

"No. Look, more clouds are gathering in the sky. It will rain again, Sulekha. Let's wait."

"Clouds are gathering in your heart too, my dear. You know, there was a day when clouds gathered in my heart as well. Even darker clouds."

"That day? Which day, Sulekha?"

Sulekha didn't answer. She walked toward the small rooftop garden and continued past it until she reached the very edge of the roof. There was no railing on that side, and the remaining part of the wall was weak and fragile. The plaster had become so loose that even the touch of a bird might make it fall. Still, Sulekha didn't stop. She had no reason to. She took a few more steps and began floating in the air. After all, she had been able to float like that since that very day.

The man followed her, as if something was pulling him toward her. He was growing restless.

"Teach me how to float like you, Sulekha. Tell me, which day did those clouds gather in your heart? Please, tell me."

But Sulekha said nothing. She kept floating here and there, moving with the wind like a bird. Without thinking much, the restless man began running toward her. He wanted to reach Sulekha. He wanted to float in the air just like her. But he couldn't. Instead, a sudden, heavy thud came from below, breaking the silence of the evening. Within moments, his white panjabi turned red.

"Monsoor, clouds gathered in my heart the day I saw you with another woman. The day you pushed me from this very spot and made me fall. Perhaps it's better this way. Now we can both float in the air, together, around the courtyard of this empty house."

The man had finally received Sulekha's answer. But he could no longer reply. His vision slowly began to blur. And then, the rain started again. Heavy rain. Gentle, rhythmic rain. Rain that took away all the silence of the evening once more. He was completely drenched.

Horror and ThrillerThe Window OppositeMay 12, 2026Read ↗

1.

It was getting late into the night. The yellow glow of the sodium streetlights around Sombraluz Apartments seemed brighter than ever. There was a large tree right beside my window. Every now and then, strong winds swept through the gray, mountainous city of El Paso, shaking the leaves and casting strange shadows on the windowpane. Sometimes, it looked as though someone was standing on the other side. Or perhaps someone was slowly walking past my window.

At first, I didn't pay much attention to it. But gradually, something else began to make me uneasy. The apartment directly opposite mine seemed rather strange. Its curtains were always closed during the day, while at night, the lights would occasionally come on. Yet, in the three months I had lived there, I had never seen anyone enter or leave that apartment. I had never seen anyone on its balcony either. Every night, at exactly 3 a.m., the kitchen light would come on. Soft music would play for a while, followed by the sounds of someone cooking. Then the light would go out.

2.

One night, I suddenly woke up. It was exactly 3 a.m. I pulled the curtain aside and looked outside. As usual, a soft yellow light came on in the apartment opposite mine. For no particular reason, I kept staring at it. There was only a thin curtain over the window, and I could make out a faint shadow moving behind it. It looked as though someone was standing in front of the sink.

Motionless.

For a long time.

Then, suddenly, the figure slowly turned its head toward me. It was enormous, with eyes that seemed to glow like fire. There was something mysterious about its appearance. A sudden chill ran through me, and I immediately pulled the curtain closed.

But a moment later, I couldn't help laughing at myself. I found it almost ridiculous that, at my age, I could still be frightened by something as simple as a shadow.

3.

After that night, I stopped going near the window as often. Some time had passed. One day, I met an elderly Mexican woman from a neighboring apartment in the laundry room for the first time. She was short, with white hair. Her name was Isabella.

While we were talking, she suddenly asked, "Do you still leave your window open at night?"

I was a little surprised.

"Yes, sometimes. Why?"

Isabella remained silent for a moment. Then, in a calm, matter-of-fact voice, she said, "Many people used to, at first."

I laughed.

"What do you mean?"

She didn't answer. As she put her clothes into the laundry basket, she simply said, "If they stare at you through the window at night, let them stare. But never wave at them."

My smile slowly faded.

"They? Who are you talking about?"

This time, she looked directly at me with tired eyes.

"The ones on the other side of the window."

4.

I couldn't sleep that night. The old woman's words kept coming back to me. Who were they? What was she trying to tell me? With all these questions running through my mind, I found myself pulling the curtain aside without even realizing it.

It was exactly 3 a.m.

The same sodium lights were glowing around Sombraluz. Everything was quiet and still. As usual, a faint light came on in the apartment opposite mine. A shadow stood behind the curtain. But tonight, there seemed to be two shadows instead of one.

The second shadow looked a lot like Isabella, the woman I had met in the laundry room.

I was about to look away when I noticed something. The shadow was waving at me.

Smiling.

As if under some strange spell, I raised my hand and waved back.

But the moment I remembered her warning, my heart suddenly sank. Her words began echoing in my ears.

"Let them stare, but never wave at them. Never wave at them..."

5.

I woke up to the sound of birds chirping and sunlight streaming through my window. The curtain was still pulled aside.

A small crowd had gathered outside Isabella's apartment. A priest was conducting a funeral service beside a coffin.

Isabella was dead.

She had died at 1 a.m. that morning.

I also asked the apartment manager about the unit opposite mine.

Apparently, it had been empty for the past four months.

No one lived there.

Horror and ThrillerThe Empty BenchJune 2, 2026Read ↗

It had been raining all day. Although the rain stopped in the afternoon, dark clouds still covered the sky. Along the wet roads, flowers of different colors were blooming—purple, pink, yellow, orange, and white. I had never seen it rain for so long in this city before. Everything around us felt unusually calm and peaceful.

The four of us went out that afternoon: Shafiq, Muhib Bhai, Raju Bhai, and me. We had McDonald's coffee and a bag of French fries with us. First, we stopped at the small park beside the nursing college. The grass was wet, the air was cool after the rain, and the trees and plants looked fresh and bright green. Raju Bhai was busy taking pictures, one after another. Shafiq sat on a slightly wet bench, one leg crossed over the other, holding a cup of coffee. Muhib Bhai quietly looked up at the sky. In the distance, dark clouds were drifting around the mountains. At one point, Maruf Bhai came up in our conversation. We had tried several times to convince him to join us, but he hadn't come.

"Rain always makes him sad. I wonder who it reminds him of," Shafiq said with a laugh. We all laughed along.

Shortly before sunset, we walked toward the Mexican View Point in the park. The place looked unusually beautiful after the rain. Below us, the entire city of Juárez was glowing with the lights of streets and houses. The Mexican city seemed so close that it felt as though we could reach out and touch all the stars in the sky. We could clearly see the railway tracks running along the border. A long freight train was passing by, blowing its horn as it moved forward.

The flowers growing beside the railing swayed continuously in the cool wind. Suddenly, a beautiful, unfamiliar fragrance filled the air. Was it the smell of burning incense? Or perhaps a scented candle? But there was no one around except us. Could it be the fragrance of jasmine or tuberose? Did those flowers even grow in this city?

Raju Bhai stretched out both arms in his usual way and said, "Look, Shafiq! We have the entire viewpoint to ourselves today. Want to go to Mexico again?" Shafiq took a sip of his coffee and replied, "No, Bhai. I learned my lesson the first time. If I ever go again, I'm not going without you. Besides, on such a beautiful golden evening, how could I go anywhere alone without my brothers?" We all burst into laughter. Suddenly, Muhib Bhai looked toward a bench at the far end of the park and asked, "Is someone sitting over there?"

We looked in that direction. The bench was completely empty. But the grass in front of it seemed a little flattened, as though someone had been standing there for a long time.

"Yes, Bhai, your companion is sitting there. Why don't you go and keep them company?" Raju Bhai joked. The rest of us laughed too. Muhib Bhai didn't say anything else. Meanwhile, the incense-like smell was growing stronger. Once it got dark, we all returned to our homes.

Two days later, Raju Bhai shared the pictures from the park in our Messenger group. At first, I didn't notice anything unusual. Wet railings, mountains and clouds, coffee cups in our hands, flowers in the background, and our smiling faces. A beautiful rain-soaked evening with the city of Juárez behind us. But suddenly, one picture caught my attention. The bench at the far end of the park was visible in the photograph. And someone was actually sitting there. Wearing a hoodie, head lowered, face blurred. Strangely enough, the person looked like Maruf Bhai.

I stared at the picture silently for a while. Then I wrote in the group chat, "Can you guys see someone sitting on the bench in this picture?"

For several minutes, nobody replied. Then Muhib Bhai sent a message.

"Didn't I tell you someone was sitting on that bench? Doesn't the person look like Maruf Bhai?"

Maruf Bhai saw the message. He even reacted to Muhib Bhai's message with a like. Then he replied, "Last year, something unusual happened at the tree in front of that bench. Nobody goes there after sunset anymore. You did the right thing by leaving before it got completely dark."

I replied, "What kind of unusual incident?"

He saw my message, but there was no reply. I had a feeling he wouldn't reply. Just then, that same sweet fragrance suddenly drifted into my room again. The smell of burning incense, or perhaps jasmine and tuberose. Someone knocked on the door.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

I slowly walked toward it. The moment I looked through the peephole, I froze...

PoetryEmptinessJanuary 11, 2026Read ↗

I am far away,
across the ocean,
on the other side of the world.
Where
night has forgotten how to disappear into day.
Where
the lights along the streets and inside homes glow like stars.

The sky is vast,
clear, bright, and blue.
Yet none of that blue feels familiar.
It feels like a borrowed roof,
beneath which I am only a guest.

The shadows of clouds fall upon the Franklin Mountains,
wrapping themselves around the darkness of my sorrow.
I remember—
I have left behind
the way I speak,
the familiar sounds of home,
the nicknames that belonged to home.

In this new city, the day arrives
with the heavy clang of an iron door.
No birds sing,
no call to prayer is heard,
no children play,
no afternoon dust settles anywhere.
Even the balcony seems untouched by time.

The stove is lit,
food cooks over a blazing flame,
yet there is no taste, no satisfaction—
no familiar touch.

I am afraid even to open my diary.
It feels as though
these last few days
are falling away
like folded sheets of paper.

It feels as though
the shuttle window is trembling again,
the mountain road twists and turns,
the birds of Katapahar
are calling out, recognizing me,
and a layer of mist is holding my hand.

Across the ocean, there was a home,
there were conversations,
stories and arguments.
Beautiful, sweet arguments.

To me,
even an argument meant I would always return,
like a pigeon finding its way back to its nest
at the end of the day.

And yet—
that pigeon has now become a migratory bird.

Its days drift away
like a kite flown by a boy on an afternoon.
Sometimes returning,
sometimes finding freedom,
sometimes caught in the pull of another kite,
trapped somewhere in the open sky.

At the end of the day, I find myself standing
on a distant shore,
where waves keep coming,
but the words for returning never come.

Where living becomes
one long wait,
searching for a nest,
flying toward my roots.
